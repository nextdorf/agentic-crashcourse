# Crash Course Instructions

- These instructions apply to Markdown chapters in this directory.
- Write primarily for readers: both workshop participants revisiting the material and people discovering the repository independently. The same chapters should still support a live presentation.
- Prefer a concrete demo, workflow, or example before abstract explanation.
- Keep main chapters practical and focused, but do not cut useful reasoning merely to fit presentation timing.
- Include practical artifacts where useful: prompts, commands, config snippets, file names, screenshots to discuss, or expected outcomes.
- Call out traps, tradeoffs, and failure modes when they affect how someone should use the tool.
- Move broad surveys, niche comparisons, provider opinions, and deep rabbit holes to appendix or further reading material.
- Preserve historical prompts verbatim, including typos, informal language, and references to local or gitignored files.

## Tone, Style, And Teaching Approach

- Use direct, natural language. Avoid detached framing such as "according to the documentation" when the fact can be stated directly and followed by its source.
- Use first person for the author's own choices, experiences, and opinions. Do not disguise subjective judgment as neutral consensus.
- An informal, opinionated voice is welcome when it makes the point clearer. Phrases such as "magic trick", "generic design slop", or "incomprehensible mess" fit better than sanitized corporate prose.
- Address the reader rather than writing presenter notes. Do not describe what the workshop should make the audience feel; create the effect through concrete results, relatable risks, contrast, and narrative.
- Keep commands and reproduction steps in the chapter even when they are also used during the live presentation.
- Explain why an example works, then show its limitations. Treat useful techniques as potential double-edged swords instead of presenting them as universal best practices.
- Give important warnings enough space and visual weight. Bold the central sentence when the reader should not miss it, then explain the practical consequences.
- Prefer specific claims tied to the current example over generic advice. Name the files, decisions, failure modes, and consequences involved.
- Preserve detailed walkthroughs when they help a reader understand the actual process. Do not replace them with summaries, learning outcomes, presenter instructions, or classroom exercises unless explicitly requested.
- Use memorable section titles and metaphors when they sharpen the underlying idea. If a metaphor is introduced, carry it through the relevant section instead of mentioning it once and abandoning it.
- Keep paragraphs readable, but do not make the prose unnaturally terse. The intended style combines compact practical material with enough explanation to make the reasoning stand on its own.
- When the user adds remarks enclosed in `<...>`, treat them as editing instructions unless the surrounding text clearly shows they belong in the published Markdown. Apply the instruction and remove the remark.
